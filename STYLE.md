# Editorial Style

This file contains reusable editorial rules for published articles. Apply the
rules when they fit the language, audience, and publication target in the
brief. Article-specific human feedback remains in that run's review artifact.

## Reader-facing prose

- Write natural prose for the reader, not narration about the writing process.
- Prefer plain, concrete wording over abstract or machine-like expressions.
- Vary sentence endings and rhythm instead of mechanically repeating the same
  tense or construction.
- Do not use the Japanese em dash (`——`) in Japanese prose.
- Do not expose internal outline labels such as “pillar” in published headings
  unless the term is natural and meaningful to the audience.

## Quoting and paraphrasing sources

- Set the attribution frame before the quoted or paraphrased content ("in the
  next sentence, he writes: ..."), not after it. A trailing demonstrative such
  as "the next sentence is this" can be misread as pointing forward to the
  following paragraph instead of back at the quotation.

## Avoid artificial signposting

- State the promise directly instead of describing it as a contract with the
  reader.
- Do not tease withheld answers with phrases such as “the answer will come
  later.”
- Do not command readers to continue, skip, or react in a particular way.

## Evidence and links

- Place source links close to the claims they support.
- Keep internal research mechanics, such as fetch failures, in evidence
  artifacts unless the limitation materially changes the reader's conclusion.
- A source appendix is optional and does not replace links near relevant
  claims.

## Assertion and attribution

- State verified facts plainly: the existence and content of a cited post, the
  existence of a product or price, and statements on official pages are facts.
  Do not soften them with hearsay endings such as "〜とされる".
- When a statement's truth cannot be independently verified, attribute it
  ("the author writes that ..."). Attribution is a form of assertion, not a
  reservation, and it does not need further hedging.
- Do not expose verification labels (Fact, Observation, Inference, Unresolved)
  in reader-facing prose. They belong in the claim ledger and evidence
  artifacts.
- Concentrate unresolved items in one dedicated section instead of scattering
  caveats through the body. Outside that section, keep reservations rare, at
  most about one per section.

## Clarity over rhetoric

- Do not replace explanation with compressed rhetorical figures, such as
  numeric contrasts whose categories overlap or whose expectation was never
  established. If a clever construction is less clear than plain prose, write
  plain prose.

## Time-bound material

- Disclose the observation window of the article's source material when most
  of it predates the publication date by months.
- Distinguish time-stamped particulars (metrics, tool versions, tactical
  details) from date-independent structural points. Date the former; let the
  latter stand without dates.
- State periods and totals precisely. Do not convert a cumulative snapshot
  into a rate, or stretch an interval between two posts into continuous
  effort.
- When a source discloses favorable and unfavorable figures together, keep
  them together. Do not excerpt only the flattering half of a disclosure.

## Applying examples and feedback

- Treat example rewrites as illustrations of a principle, not mandatory stock
  phrases.
- Keep one-off corrections in the run-specific human review. Promote a rule to
  this file only when it should guide future articles too.
